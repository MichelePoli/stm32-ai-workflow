\section{Large Language Models}
\glspl{llm} represent a transformative shift in artificial intelligence, moving from specialized pattern matchers to versatile reasoning engines capable of understanding and generating human-like language across diverse domains. These models are designed to capture the structural and semantic nuances of text, allowing them to perform complex tasks such as reasoning, summarization, and code synthesis. In the context of MLOps, LLMs serve as intelligent orchestrators, capable of interpreting high-level user requirements and translating them into executable workflows or hardware-specific configurations.

\subsection{Evolution of Language Modeling}
The development of modern \glspl{llm} scales from early statistical approaches to the massive neural architectures of today \cite{wang2024historydevelopmentprincipleslarge}. Traditional language models relied on N-grams and the Markov assumption to predict the next word based on a limited history, often suffering from sparse data and a lack of long-range coherence. The introduction of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units allowed for better temporal modeling, but it was the \textit{Transformer} architecture \cite{transformers} that truly unlocked the current era of LLMs.

By leveraging the self-attention mechanism, Transformers enabled parallel processing and the capture of global dependencies within a text. Following the success of GPT-2 \cite{radford2019gpt2} and GPT-3 \cite{brown2020gpt3}, the industry observed "scaling laws," which suggested that increasing model parameters, data volume, and compute budget leads to predictable improvements in capability. This trend was further refined by compute-optimal strategies, such as those proposed in the Chinchilla study \cite{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, and by alignment techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which ensure models are helpful, honest, and harmless (HHH) \cite{openai2022instructgpt, chung2022flan, touvron2023llama}. The exponential growth of these models over the last few years is visualized in Fig. \ref{fig:llmevo}.

\subsection{The Attention Mechanism}
The core innovation of the Transformer is the self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to dynamically weight the importance of different tokens in a sequence relative to one another. Unlike sequential models, self-attention computes pairwise interactions between all tokens simultaneously, effectively bypassing the limitations of fixed context windows.

Mathematically, the mechanism uses three linear projections of the input embeddings: Queries ($Q$), Keys ($K$), and Values ($V$). The attention score is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the dimensionality of the keys, used as a scaling factor to maintain gradient stability \cite{transformers}. Multi-head attention extends this by running several attention layers in parallel, allowing the model to focus on different aspects of the input representation (e.g., syntax vs. semantics) concurrently \cite{soydaner2022attention}.

\subsection{Training and Fine-Tuning Paradigms}
The development of a production-ready LLM typically follows a three-stage pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-training:} The model is trained on massive, unlabeled text corpora using self-supervised objectives (e.g., Causal Language Modeling or Masked Language Modeling) to learn general linguistic patterns and factual knowledge \cite{johnson2024primerlargelanguagemodels, micheletti2024explorationmaskedcausallanguage}.
    \item \textbf{Fine-tuning:} The pre-trained model is adapted to domain-specific tasks using smaller, high-quality labeled datasets. This improves accuracy in specialized areas like medical diagnosis or firmware development \cite{pretraining}.
    \item \textbf{Instruction Tuning:} The model is trained to follow specific natural language instructions or "prompts." This stage enables zero-shot generalization, where the model can perform new tasks simply by following a well-defined prompt \cite{instructiontuning}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{State-of-the-Art and Industry Landscape}
As of 2025, the LLM ecosystem is dominated by a few key players, each focusing on different optimization goals. OpenAI with the GPT-o series (including o1 and o3) has emphasized advanced reasoning and chain-of-thought capabilities. Anthropic's Claude 3.5 and 3.7 models are renowned for their coding precision and "Claude Artifacts" UI for document interaction. Google’s Gemini 3 Pro \cite{google2025gemini3} has recently emerged as a primary leader, surpassing many competitors in technical benchmarks—achieving a record 91.9\% on GPQA Diamond—while scaling massive 1M token context windows and native multimodality. However, the prevailing paradigm of monolithic "super models" is being challenged by the concept of \textit{collective intelligence}, where the orchestrated routing and aggregation of multiple open-source models can surpass proprietary giants while significantly reducing inference costs \cite{tang2026beyond}. This shift underscores the importance of agentic orchestration frameworks, which treat models as modular components rather than isolated black boxes. Meanwhile, open-source initiatives like Mistral and Meta’s Llama 3/4 families provide accessible, high-performance models that can be deployed locally via frameworks like Ollama, fostering reproducibility and data privacy in research \cite{gpt4_technical_report, palm_2022, mistral_7b}.

\subsection{Limitations and Deployment Challenges}
Despite their power, LLMs possess inherent flaws. Hallucination—the generation of plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information—remains a significant hurdle for high-stakes MLOps \cite{limitationsLLM}. Models also lack true causal reasoning, relying instead on statistical patterns in their training data. Furthermore, the high computational cost of running large-scale models often necessitates a hybrid approach, where lightweight local models (e.g., Llama-3-8B) handle routine tasks while larger proprietary APIs are reserved for complex reasoning. This thesis addresses these limitations through Agentic Orchestration and RAG, ensuring that the LLM's outputs are grounded in hardware-specific documentation and validated through execution loops.
